🌍 Ecology: Detailed Study Notes

1. Meaning of Ecology

Ecology is the branch of biology that studies the relationships between living organisms and their physical environment.

It examines how organisms interact with one another and with factors such as light, temperature, water, soil, air, and nutrients.

2. Levels of Ecological Organization

Ecological organization can be arranged from simple to complex:

  1. Organism: A single living individual.
  2. Population: Organisms of the same species living in an area.
  3. Community: Different populations living and interacting together.
  4. Ecosystem: A community interacting with its physical environment.
  5. Biome: A large region characterized by particular climate and vegetation.
  6. Biosphere: The part of Earth where life exists.

3. Habitat

A habitat is the natural place where an organism lives and obtains the conditions and resources necessary for survival.

Examples:

  • Fish → aquatic habitat
  • Earthworm → soil habitat
  • Monkey → forest habitat
  • Frog → freshwater and terrestrial habitats

4. Ecological Niche

An ecological niche describes the role an organism plays in its ecosystem, including how it obtains food, uses resources, and interacts with other organisms.

Two organisms may share a habitat but have different niches.

5. Abiotic Factors

Abiotic factors are the non-living components of an ecosystem.

  • ☀️ Light intensity
  • 🌡️ Temperature
  • 💧 Water availability
  • 🌧️ Rainfall
  • 🌬️ Wind
  • 🌱 Soil type
  • 🧂 Mineral salts
  • 💨 Humidity
  • ⚗️ Soil pH

These factors influence where organisms can live, their growth, reproduction, and survival.

6. Biotic Factors

Biotic factors are the living components of an ecosystem.

  • Plants
  • Animals
  • Fungi
  • Bacteria
  • Other microorganisms

Organisms interact through competition, predation, parasitism, mutualism, and other relationships.

7. Population

A population is a group of organisms of the same species occupying a particular area at a particular time.

Population size can change because of:

  • Births
  • Deaths
  • Immigration
  • Emigration

Population change = Births + Immigration − Deaths − Emigration

8. Community

A community consists of different populations of organisms living and interacting within the same area.

For example, a forest community may contain trees, grasses, insects, birds, fungi, bacteria, and mammals.

9. Ecosystem

An ecosystem consists of living organisms and the non-living environment with which they interact.

Ecosystems may be natural or artificial.

  • 🌳 Forest ecosystem
  • 🏞️ Grassland ecosystem
  • 🌊 Pond ecosystem
  • 🌊 Marine ecosystem
  • 🌾 Agricultural ecosystem

10. Producers, Consumers and Decomposers

🌱 Producers

Producers make their own food, usually through photosynthesis. Green plants and algae are common examples.

🐇 Consumers

Consumers obtain energy by feeding on other organisms.

  • Primary consumers → herbivores
  • Secondary consumers → feed on primary consumers
  • Tertiary consumers → feed on other consumers

🍄 Decomposers

Decomposers break down dead organisms and organic materials, releasing nutrients back into the environment.

Examples include bacteria and fungi.

11. Food Chain

A food chain shows the transfer of food energy from one organism to another.

🌱 Grass → 🦗 Grasshopper → 🐸 Frog → 🐍 Snake → 🦅 Hawk

The arrows show the direction of energy transfer.

12. Food Web

A food web is a network of interconnected food chains within an ecosystem.

Food webs usually provide a more realistic representation of feeding relationships because organisms often have more than one food source.

13. Energy Flow in an Ecosystem

Energy enters most ecosystems from the Sun. Plants capture some of this energy during photosynthesis.

Energy is then transferred through different feeding levels. Some energy is lost as heat during metabolic activities.

Therefore, energy flow through an ecosystem is generally one-way.

14. Ecological Pyramids

Ecological pyramids represent relationships between trophic levels.

  • Pyramid of numbers: Shows the number of organisms at each trophic level.
  • Pyramid of biomass: Shows the total mass of organisms at each trophic level.
  • Pyramid of energy: Shows the amount of energy available at each trophic level.

15. Ecological Relationships

  • Predation: One organism kills and feeds on another.
  • Competition: Organisms compete for limited resources.
  • Parasitism: One organism benefits while the host is harmed.
  • Mutualism: Both organisms benefit.
  • Commensalism: One benefits while the other is generally unaffected.

16. Biodiversity

Biodiversity refers to the variety of living organisms in an area or on Earth.

It includes:

  • Genetic diversity
  • Species diversity
  • Ecosystem diversity

17. Conservation

Conservation involves protecting and managing natural resources and living organisms so they can continue to exist and reproduce.

Examples include:

  • 🌳 Afforestation
  • 🦁 Protection of wildlife
  • ♻️ Recycling
  • 🌱 Establishment of protected areas
  • 🚫 Prevention of illegal hunting

18. Pollution

Pollution is the introduction of harmful substances or forms of energy into the environment.

  • Air pollution
  • Water pollution
  • Soil pollution
  • Noise pollution

Pollution can affect organisms, habitats, food chains, biodiversity, and human health.

19. Quick Ecology Revision

  • Ecology: Study of organisms and their environment.
  • Habitat: Place where an organism lives.
  • Niche: Role of an organism in its ecosystem.
  • Population: Members of one species in an area.
  • Community: Different populations interacting.
  • Ecosystem: Community plus its physical environment.
  • Producer: Makes its own food.
  • Consumer: Obtains food from other organisms.
  • Decomposer: Breaks down dead organic matter.
  • Biodiversity: Variety of life.

🌍 Ecology: Advanced Study Notes

20. Population Density

Population density is the number of individuals of a species living in a given area or volume.

Population Density = Number of organisms ÷ Area occupied

Population density can be affected by food availability, predation, disease, competition, climate, and availability of suitable habitats.

21. Factors Affecting Population Size

  • Birth rate: Increases population size.
  • Death rate: Decreases population size.
  • Immigration: Movement of organisms into a population.
  • Emigration: Movement of organisms out of a population.
  • Competition: Reduces access to limited resources.
  • Predation: Can reduce prey populations.
  • Disease: Can increase mortality.

22. Carrying Capacity

Carrying capacity is the maximum population size that an environment can support sustainably with the available resources.

If a population exceeds the carrying capacity, competition for resources may increase and population size may decline.

23. Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the gradual change in the composition and structure of a biological community over time.

Primary Succession

Primary succession begins in an area where there was previously little or no established soil, such as newly exposed rock.

Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs where an existing community has been disturbed but soil remains.

24. Carbon Cycle

Carbon continuously moves between organisms and the physical environment.

  • Plants remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere during photosynthesis.
  • Animals obtain carbon by feeding on plants or other animals.
  • Respiration returns carbon dioxide to the atmosphere.
  • Decomposition returns carbon to the environment.
  • Combustion releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

25. Water Cycle

The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land, oceans, and living organisms.

  • Evaporation: Liquid water changes into water vapour.
  • Transpiration: Plants release water vapour.
  • Condensation: Water vapour forms tiny water droplets.
  • Precipitation: Water falls as rain or other forms of precipitation.
  • Infiltration: Water enters the soil.
  • Runoff: Water flows over the land surface.

26. Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is essential for the production of proteins and nucleic acids in living organisms.

Atmospheric nitrogen cannot be used directly by most organisms. Certain microorganisms help convert nitrogen into forms that plants can absorb.

  • Nitrogen fixation
  • Nitrification
  • Assimilation
  • Ammonification
  • Denitrification

27. Trophic Levels

A trophic level represents the feeding position occupied by an organism in a food chain or food web.

  1. Producers
  2. Primary consumers
  3. Secondary consumers
  4. Tertiary consumers

Energy generally decreases as it moves from one trophic level to the next because organisms use energy for life processes and much of it is released as heat.

28. Human Activities and Ecosystems

Human activities can significantly change natural ecosystems.

  • Deforestation
  • Mining
  • Urbanization
  • Industrial activities
  • Agriculture
  • Overfishing
  • Pollution
  • Introduction of invasive species

29. Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal of forests, often to obtain land for agriculture, settlements, roads, or other human activities.

Possible effects include:

  • Loss of habitats
  • Reduction in biodiversity
  • Soil erosion
  • Changes in the water cycle
  • Increased carbon dioxide in the atmosphere

30. Climate Change and Ecosystems

Changes in climate can affect temperature, rainfall patterns, habitats, species distributions, and the timing of biological events.

Species may respond by changing their distribution, behaviour, or seasonal activities. Species that cannot adapt or relocate may face increased ecological pressure.

31. Conservation Methods

In-situ Conservation

Protecting organisms within their natural habitats.

Examples include national parks, wildlife reserves, and protected forests.

Ex-situ Conservation

Protecting organisms outside their natural habitats.

Examples include zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, and gene banks.
